This is the demo from the micrograd README: a and b go in.
The expression is meaningless. It only shows what the engine can do.
Run it forward and out comes one number, g.
Backward asks: how fast does g change if we nudge a or b?
Nudge a up a little and g grows about 138.8 times as fast.
Check it: nudge a by h = 0.001 and see how far g really moves.
A bigger h gives 139.0388; as h shrinks it closes in on 138.8338.
Backprop works on any expression. Neural nets are a calmer one.
micrograd uses single numbers on purpose; tensors only add speed.
This tiny network has sixteen knobs; real ones have millions.
In code, every knob is just a number.
The loss measures how wrong we are. Lower is better.
Turn one knob blindly and the mistake grows: wrong way!
Another knob, other direction: lucky this time, the loss falls.
But luck will not scale to thousands of knobs. We need a direction.
Here is our plan: twelve short chapters, one idea each.
First slopes, several inputs, and the computation graph.
Then the backward pass, one neuron, and automating it.
Next, adding up gradients, more operations, and PyTorch.
Finally a whole network, training it, and a loss surface.
Every chapter has three acts, so you see each idea three times.
A uses the lecture's numbers; B changes them and watches what moves.
The expert corner adds what a specialist would warn you about.
Chapter 1 answers it, starting with simple slopes.
