Example A: the lecture's own function, a parabola we can plot.
Forty x values from -5 up in steps of 0.25, each with its f(x).
Slope tells how steeply the curve climbs when we step right.
Pick x = 3. First, plain arithmetic: what is f(3)?
Predict: if x moves a bit right of 3, is f above or below 20?
Above: the curve climbs. White is the step h, orange the rise.
Zoom in: a line through two curve points is a secant.
Now shrink h. The secant turns into the tangent, which only touches.
Slope is rise over run; the run from x to x+h is just h.
Real numbers in the formula: nudge by h = 0.001 and divide by h.
Predict: at x = -3, will the slope be positive or negative?
Sliding left, the readout passes white at the bottom, then turns orange.
Sliding right, the slope shrinks toward 0 near the bottom.
Near x = 2/3 the tangent is flat: slope about 0, nudges do nothing.
Warning: too many zeros in h and floats run out of digits.
So the derivative is the slope at one point: rise over run, h tiny.
Example B: what if we change the function? A cubic, then a kink.
Same idea, new curve g of x: it has a hump and a valley.
Predict: where on this curve is the tangent perfectly flat?
The slope reads about 0 at x = -1 (hump top) and x = 1 (valley).
The table shows slope 0 only at x = -1 and 1; elsewhere it is 9 or -3.
Now the absolute value: a V with a sharp corner at x = 0.
Predict: what single slope does the corner have at x = 0?
Two answers: from the left the secant gives -1, from the right +1.
Shrink h: the secants never agree, so no derivative exists at 0.
The corner has no slope. Now back to our parabola, to try a huge h.
Back on f: if h is far too big, the secant leaves the tangent.
With h = 1 the slope reads 17, not 14: the step is too coarse.
Expert corner: how good is this nudge, and can we do better?
We measure the error against the true slope 14, for h = 10^-k.
Predict: at h = 0.0001, which formula lands closer to 14?
Blue forward step looks only right; teal central looks both sides.
At k = 4 the forward error is about 3e-4, the central one about 4e-11.
So micrograd never nudges: each operation knows its exact slope.
In code, central costs one more call of f but is far more exact.
Errors shrink to k = 8, then rounding noise wins.
At k = 16 both slopes read 0, so the error is the full 14.
