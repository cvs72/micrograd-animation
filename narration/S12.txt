Example A: one tanh neuron with two weights, w1 and w2, and bias 0.5.
Every pair (w1, w2) gives one loss: the height above that spot.
At the start (-2.5, 2) the engine adds four squared errors.
Doing that for every grid point builds a landscape: low is good.
Contour lines on the floor join points with equal loss, like a map.
Our ball starts at (-2.5, 2). Which way should it roll?
The gradient (orange) points uphill; we step the opposite way (teal).
One update: w1 minus learning rate times its slope.
Now 25 steps: each one re-asks the engine for the slope, then moves.
Seen from above, the path crosses contours toward the lower rings.
Example B: what if we start elsewhere, or take much bigger steps?
Three starting points, each tried with a small and a huge step.
Small steps: the ball slides down and settles in the valley.
Predict: will a step twenty times bigger get there sooner?
No: it overshoots the valley and lands higher, worse than before.
Huge steps fly off the map; the small ones always finish lower.
Expert corner: the gradient is perpendicular to the contour lines.
Gradient arrows on the map cross every ring at right angles.
Check one ring: slope and contour meet at about 90 degrees.
But in a saturated corner the surface is flat: slope almost zero.
Twenty-five steps and the ball has hardly moved: the plateau traps it.
Real networks have millions of weights: no surface to draw.
Same idea: one slope per weight, at every single point.
The bowl is an analogy; the downhill rule is the real thing.
